# Working safely with high voltage

Electric vehicles remove petrol and hot exhausts from the workshop, and add a different hazard: large amounts of stored electrical energy. A two-wheeler pack can deliver hundreds of amps into a short circuit. Treating it casually because “it’s only 48 volts” is how technicians get burnt and packs catch fire.

> **Important.** This chapter explains principles. It is not a substitute for formal high-voltage training, your employer’s safety procedures or the manufacturer’s service instructions. Follow those first.

## Two kinds of hazard

**Electric shock** depends mainly on voltage. Vehicle standards treat DC systems above 60 V as a higher voltage class that needs protection against direct contact, and many two-wheeler packs, at 60 V and 72 V nominal and higher when fully charged, are at or above that line. Four-wheeler systems at several hundred volts can be lethal.

**Short circuits and arcs** depend mainly on current, and that hazard exists at any pack voltage. A spanner bridging two terminals of even a low-voltage pack can weld itself in place, throw molten metal, burn the person holding it and damage cells. Rings, watches and metal bracelets can do the same against skin.

**Thermal runaway** is the third risk. A damaged, overcharged or internally shorted lithium cell can overheat, vent flammable gas and ignite, setting off neighbouring cells. These fires are intense, produce toxic smoke and can reignite hours later.

## Before you start work

- **Know the system.** Identify the pack, the high-voltage cables (often orange on four-wheelers) and the isolation point from the service manual.
- **Wear the right protection.** Safety glasses always. Insulated gloves rated for the system voltage when working near live parts, inspected before each use.
- **Remove metal from hands and wrists.**
- **Use insulated tools** where live parts may be present.
- **Keep the area dry and tidy**, with nothing conductive lying across terminals.

## Isolating the vehicle

Make the system safe before touching high-voltage parts:

1. Switch the vehicle off and remove the key or disable it remotely.
2. Disconnect or remove the battery pack, or operate the service disconnect on vehicles that have one.
3. **Wait.** Motor controllers contain capacitors that hold charge after the pack is disconnected. Follow the manufacturer’s waiting time.
4. **Prove it is dead.** Test your meter on a known live source, test the circuit you will work on, then test the meter on the known source again. This live, dead, live check confirms the meter did not fail in the middle.
5. **Lock and tag.** Make sure no one can reconnect the pack while you are working. Keep the removed pack or service plug under your control.

## Handling battery packs

- Lift and carry packs properly; they are heavy, and a dropped pack can be damaged internally with no visible sign.
- Never open a pack, pierce cells or bypass a BMS unless you are trained and authorised to repair packs.
- Protect terminals and connectors with caps when a pack is out of the vehicle.
- Charge only with the specified charger, in a ventilated area away from flammable materials, with smoke detection where packs charge in numbers.

## Damaged and suspect packs

Treat any pack that has been dropped hard, crushed, submerged, overheated or punctured as potentially dangerous, even if it seems to work. Warning signs include swelling, hissing, a sweet or chemical smell, smoke, leaking fluid or heat while not in use.

- Move it, if safe, to a designated quarantine area outdoors or in a fire-resistant container, away from other packs and buildings.
- Do not charge or use it.
- Label it and follow the procedure for safe storage, transport and disposal.

## If a pack catches fire

Raise the alarm and get people out first. A lithium battery fire is difficult to extinguish and can reignite. Follow your site’s emergency procedure, call the fire service, and do not attempt to fight a large fire. Large amounts of water are generally used to cool burning packs and neighbouring cells; small extinguishers mainly buy time to evacuate.

## Regulations

In India, battery safety for electric two- and three-wheelers is governed by the AIS-156 standard, which was strengthened after a series of fires in 2022 with tighter requirements on cell testing, BMS protection and thermal propagation. Workshops and fleets should keep up with the current version and with any requirements from their state and the vehicle manufacturer.

> **In the field.** Make safety visible and routine: a marked charging area, a quarantine bin for suspect packs, gloves and glasses at every bench, and a five-minute briefing when a new technician joins. The packs that cause incidents are usually the ones someone thought were probably fine.
