# Field quality: from failure to lasting fix

Repair restores one vehicle. Field quality makes sure the same failure does not happen to the next thousand. It is the discipline of turning field evidence into root causes, corrective actions and, where needed, design changes. This chapter covers the methods and, just as important, how to make them stick.

## Correction, corrective action and preventive action

These three terms are often mixed up:

- **Correction** fixes the immediate problem: replace the failed part, repair the vehicle.
- **Corrective action** removes the cause so the same failure does not recur.
- **Preventive action** removes the cause of a potential failure before it happens, often by applying a lesson from one part or model to others.

Together, corrective and preventive action are known as **CAPA**. A quality system that only does corrections is just a repair shop with paperwork.

## A practical sequence

The steps below follow the logic of common problem-solving frameworks such as 8D and DMAIC.

### 1. Define the problem precisely

Describe what failed, where, how often, on which vehicles, since when and how it was detected. Quantify it. “Brake failures are high” is not a problem statement. A good one names the part, the failure mode, the affected models and locations, the rate and the trend.

### 2. Contain it

If the problem affects safety or is spreading, act before the root cause is known. Containment might mean inspecting all vehicles of a model, quarantining a parts batch or adding a temporary check. Containment buys time; it is not the fix.

### 3. Find the root cause

Two simple tools do most of the work.

**5-Why** asks “why?” repeatedly until you reach a cause you can act on. For example: the connector burnt (why?) because its resistance was high (why?) because the pins were corroded (why?) because water entered (why?) because the seal was missing (why?) because the assembly procedure did not include a seal check. The last answer is actionable and points at the process, not just the part.

**Fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams** organise possible causes into categories so none are overlooked. Common categories are people, method, machine, material, measurement and environment. The team brainstorms causes under each, then uses data to rule them in or out.

Neither tool proves anything by itself. A root cause is confirmed when you can show it with evidence: the failure occurs when the cause is present, and stops when it is removed.

### 4. Choose and implement corrective actions

Good corrective actions remove or block the cause, not just make it less likely. In rough order of strength:

1. Change the design so the failure cannot happen.
2. Add a feature that makes incorrect assembly or use impossible (error-proofing).
3. Add a check that reliably detects the problem before the vehicle is used.
4. Update procedures and training.

Training alone is the weakest action, because people forget and staff change. Use it alongside stronger actions, not instead of them.

### 5. Verify effectiveness

Closing a CAPA because the action was implemented is a common mistake. Close it when the data shows the failure rate has dropped and stayed down. Decide in advance what you will measure and for how long.

### 6. Standardise and share

Update procedures, inspection checklists, design guidelines and supplier specifications so the fix becomes the standard. Apply the lesson to similar parts and models: that is the preventive half of CAPA.

## Anticipating failures with FMEA

**Failure Mode and Effects Analysis** works the other way round: instead of investigating a failure that happened, it asks what could fail. For each component or process step, the team lists possible failure modes, their effects, their causes and the controls in place, then rates severity, likelihood and detectability to prioritise action. A design FMEA (DFMEA) looks at the product; a process FMEA looks at manufacturing or service steps. Field failure data is the best input to keep an FMEA honest.

## Turning field evidence into design change

Field teams see what designers cannot: how parts behave in real heat, rain, potholes and rider behaviour. That knowledge only changes the product if it reaches the product team in a form they can use. A strong technical report includes:

- **The problem**, stated precisely, with rates and trends.
- **The evidence**: photos of failed parts, measurements, failure locations, the vehicles and conditions involved.
- **The root cause** and how it was confirmed.
- **The impact**: downtime, cost, safety and customer effect.
- **A recommendation**: what should change in the specification or design, and how its effect will be measured.

Reports that bring evidence and a clear request get acted on. Reports that bring complaints get filed.

## Warranty and supplier quality

Warranty claims are another source of field data. Each claim needs technical validation: is the failure a product defect, or the result of damage, misuse or an incorrect repair? Consistent judgement protects both the customer and the business. Patterns in validated claims, and in parts returned as faulty, are the evidence base for supplier quality discussions.

> **In the field.** The test of a quality system is simple: pick any failure that was “fixed” six months ago and check whether it is still gone. If it has returned, the root cause was not found or the action was not strong enough.
