# Common field failures and how to read them

A workshop sees vehicles one at a time. A fleet sees the same failures hundreds of times, which turns repair data into evidence. This chapter covers the failures that dominate electric two-wheeler field service and, more importantly, how to read a failed part so that the repair leads to a lasting fix.

## Where failures come from

Field failures fall into a few broad groups:

- **Wear** that is expected and should be planned for: brake pads, tyres, bearings, belts and chains, suspension bushes.
- **Environment**: water ingress, dust, heat and vibration acting on connectors, seals and electronics.
- **Use and misuse**: overloading, kerb impacts, falls, rough handling of swappable batteries, and in shared fleets, deliberate damage.
- **Design and supply**: a component that is undersized for real conditions, or a batch of parts that does not meet specification.
- **Workmanship**: an earlier repair or assembly step done incorrectly.

The repair is often the same whichever group the cause belongs to. The lasting fix is completely different. A brake plate damaged by impact needs a guard or a different mounting; one that wore out early needs a better material or a revised service interval; one from a bad batch needs a supplier conversation.

## The failures you will see most

### Brakes

Brake components wear fastest on heavy, frequently stopped vehicles. Watch for pads worn to the backing plate, cables that have stretched or corroded, and damage to brake plates and mounting hardware. Remember the electrical link: a brake that does not fully release can keep the brake switch active and cut drive, and it adds drag that drains range.

### Tyres, wheels and bearings

Punctures and low pressure are the most common single cause of downtime in many fleets. Low pressure also increases energy use and stresses hub motor bearings. Wheel bearings fail with rumbling, play in the wheel or uneven tyre wear.

### Frames and body

Impacts and falls bend stands, crack plastic panels, and in severe cases damage frames. Frame damage should always be assessed against a clear standard and tracked, because a damaged frame affects handling and safety.

### Connectors and harnesses

Burnt connectors, chafed wires and water-corroded pins (chapter 4) are among the most frequent electrical failures. They are also among the most often misdiagnosed, because their symptoms look like failed modules.

### Batteries

Deep discharge, damaged connector pins on swappable packs, casing damage from drops and weak cell groups (chapter 2).

### Motors and controllers

Hall sensor faults, burnt phase connectors, water in hub motors, bearing noise and controller transistor failures (chapter 3).

### Switches and controls

Throttles, brake switches, side stand switches and handlebar switches suffer from water and repeated use. They are cheap parts that cause expensive-looking faults.

## Reading the failed part

The part on the bench is evidence. Before it goes in the scrap bin, look at it.

**Heat marks.** Discolouration, melted plastic or a burnt smell at a connector means high resistance at that point, usually from a loose fit, corrosion or an undersized contact. If both halves are burnt, replace both.

**Corrosion.** Green or white deposits on copper and brass point to water. Ask how it got in: a damaged seal, a missing blanking plug, a pressure washer, or a design that traps water.

**Fractures.** How a metal part broke tells you why. A **fatigue** fracture, caused by repeated loading over time, usually shows a smooth area with curved “beach marks” spreading from where the crack began, and a small rough area where the part finally gave way. An **overload** fracture, from a single impact, is rough across the whole surface. Fatigue suggests a design, material or vibration issue; overload suggests impact or misuse.

**Wear patterns.** Uneven wear on pads, tyres or bearings points to misalignment, a bent component or incorrect assembly rather than normal use.

## Reading the pattern

One failure is a repair. The same failure on many vehicles is a signal. When looking at repair data, ask:

- **When does it fail?** Failures early in life (shortly after a vehicle enters service or after a repair) point to manufacturing, assembly or workmanship. Failures that rise after a certain distance or age point to wear-out. Failures spread evenly over time often point to random events like impacts. Plotted over time, these three phases form the classic “bathtub curve”.
- **Where does it fail?** If one city or site has far more of a failure, look at local conditions, routes, workshop practice or a particular parts batch.
- **Which vehicles?** A failure limited to one model, one production period or one supplier’s part narrows the cause quickly.
- **What changed?** A sudden rise often follows a change: a new part supplier, a revised procedure, the monsoon.

> **In the field.** On one shared fleet, brake plate damage kept appearing across locations. Treated as individual repairs, it looked like normal wear and tear. Treated as a pattern, the trend analysis showed a recurring failure with a common cause, and a countermeasure developed with cross-functional teams reduced it by 90%. The repair itself never changed. What changed was looking at all the repairs together.
